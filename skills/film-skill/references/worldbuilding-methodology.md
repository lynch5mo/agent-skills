# Worldbuilding Methodology

Use this when the user asks to adapt an existing story to a new setting, or when they start worldbuilding from scratch before writing scenes.

## Core Principle

**Rules before characters before scenes.** Do not start from characters or scene-by-scene mapping. Characters find their natural positions only after the world's operating rules are defined.

## The Five Foundational Questions (Q1–Q5)

When building a world from scratch, ask these in order. Do not skip ahead until the current question is settled.

### Q1: What are the basic necessities of life, and where do they come from?

Food, water, energy — their source determines everything else about the world's power structure.

Options:
- A: A central authority (factory, government, AI) produces everything. No private production exists.
- B: There is a rudimentary self-sufficiency system (farming, solar), but it's insufficient. The central authority is the supplement.
- C: Nothing is produced locally. All survival materials (including air filtration media) are refined from waste dropped from above.

### Q2: What is the direction of material exchange between layers?

- A: One-way — waste drops down. Nothing goes up.
- B: Mostly one-way, but extremely rare items flow upward through a tiny intermediary channel.
- C: Two-way cycle — waste comes down, is processed, and some form of product goes back up.

**Sub-question**: Is the waste deliberately allocated (ration system / feeding mechanism), or is it discarded accidentally (the upper layer does not know or care who it hits below)?

### Q3: What is the nature of the value equivalent (currency)?

- A: Factory tokens — the only legal currency. Only registered population can earn them. No tokens = no ration = not a legal person.
- B: Barter — valuable things are survival necessities (water filters, energy cells, intact parts). Tokens are only valid within factory facilities.
- C: Information / knowledge / access rights are also currency. Knowing when a pipe spits cargo or where a dealer makes contact can be traded for goods.

### Q4: What is the vertical social structure?

From the bottom-most layer (furthest from the upper world) to the top-most (closest to contact), describe the layers. For each layer, define:
- What they know about the upper world (rumor? hearsay? direct contact?)
- What they control (resources, information, people)
- How they relate to the layer above and below

The top layer may be a single person or a handful — not a "class" in the sociological sense. If the upper world's existence is unverifiable from below, describe how each layer's belief functions like a religion.

### Q5: What is the population's base state?

- Are there normal births and deaths, or is the population replenished from above?
- Is there a family structure, or are children registered and separated at birth?
- What is the average lifespan? What do people die from (starvation, violence, part failure, "recycling")?

## How to Use This Framework

1. Present Q1 to the user. Get a decision before moving to Q2.
2. If the user wants to jump to characters or scenes, redirect: "Let's get the world's skeleton first — characters will find their natural place once we know how this world works."
3. Do not propose character-specific details until the user asks. Q1–Q5 are about the world, not the characters.
4. Once Q1–Q5 are settled, the world's rules will naturally determine the characters' positions.

## Pitfalls

- **Do not reverse-engineer from characters.** If you start from the character, the world will feel like a costume.
- **Do not propose a single definitive answer.** Offer options and let the user choose.
- **Do not write scenes** while the Q1–Q5 framework is still open. Scene-level decisions lock in answers that should be world-level.
- **Keep a running "open questions" list.** Note dependencies and move on.
